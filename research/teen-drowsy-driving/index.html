Teen Drowsy Driving.

Practice makes better drivers. Rest helps keep them safe.

Research reviewed August 24, 2026 • Next scheduled review August 24, 2027 • U.S. safety guidance prioritized • Peer-reviewed teen and young-driver studies included

The short version: Learning to drive takes time, and every bit of experience can help a new driver grow more confident. But practice is only one part of safe driving. Knowing when to take a break matters just as much.

Fatigue is gradual

There isn’t a magic number.

Fatigue builds gradually. Some days a driver feels sharp for a long stretch. Other days they get tired much faster. Sleep, stress, time of day, traffic, weather—all of it counts.

That’s why no app or website can promise that any specific amount of time is safe. Safety is about good choices throughout the drive.

Why teen drivers need special attention

Teens are different.

Teen drivers are still building new skills, but they’re also more likely to run short on sleep. Being tired can slow reaction time and decision making, even if that person doesn’t fall asleep.

NHTSA notes that drowsy driving affects more than whether someone actually dozes off: it can reduce alertness, attention, reaction time, judgment, and decision-making ability. CDC also identifies young drivers as a group at high risk for drowsy driving, particularly in the early morning and late at night.

Practice with purpose

Building experience over time.

Early practice sessions are often shorter and focused on one or two skills. As confidence grows, families add new challenges—maybe busier roads, nighttime driving, or bad weather.

Notice what changes. It’s variety, not endurance. Many shorter drives in different conditions usually teach more than one long drive.

Experience should expand gradually: the goal is broader, appropriately supervised practice—not proving that a new driver can stay behind the wheel for a long stretch.

Breaks on longer trips

What experts recommend.

Safety organizations commonly suggest taking a break about every two hours on longer trips. That’s a reminder to stretch, switch drivers, or rest.

That isn’t a promise that shorter drives are safe. It’s a general habit to encourage regular breaks, and drivers should stop sooner whenever they feel sleepy or less alert.

How the game handles long drives

Drive Venture’s policy.

Because games can shape behavior, we choose not to reward staying behind the wheel longer. We reward variety and steady progress across many drives.

Our policy is simple: we encourage frequent practice and taking breaks.

Drive Venture will limit drives logged to two hours, and not count any time beyond 135 minutes (two hours and 15 minutes) for a single drive.

If you reach our limit—if you see a message saying we’ve stopped counting time—don’t worry. It doesn’t mean you’ve done anything wrong. It just means we’ve hit the point where we don’t want the game encouraging longer uninterrupted driving.

If you’re still on the road, just focus, finish the trip safely, and take a break before starting another long stretch.

Parker tip

Great drivers build experience over many drives, not one long one.
